Batting practice is the most repeated thing your hitters do all season, and usually the least recorded. You watch hundreds of swings a week, but by Friday most of what you saw is a feeling. A few minutes of charting turns those swings into something you can look back on, show a player, and compare next month.

This guide is for the coach standing behind the cage with a bucket nearby and one free hand. You don't need a radar gun, a stat crew, or a spreadsheet. You need a consistent habit and a clear idea of what you're writing down.

What you're actually recording

The unit of batting-practice charting is a recorded contact: one ball the hitter put in play, and where it went. It isn't an at-bat or a plate appearance, and it doesn't need to be every swing. Calling it a recorded contact keeps everyone honest. A chart of 40 recorded contacts says "here's where 40 balls went," not "here's what this kid will do in a game."

For each recorded contact, there's one thing you always capture and a few you sometimes add:

  • Always: where the ball landed or was first fielded.
  • Sometimes: how hard it was hit, the trajectory (ground ball, line drive, fly ball, pop up), and which side the pitcher threw from.
  • Rarely: a short note, such as "late on inside pitch."

The landing spot is the backbone. If you only ever record location, you'll still end up with a useful spray chart. Everything else is a bonus you add when the pace allows.

Set up before the first pitch

Charting falls apart when you're figuring out the system mid-round. Spend two minutes before practice on these:

  1. Know your order. Have the hitting groups and order written down, or entered in whatever you chart with. Most charting mistakes are a ball credited to the wrong hitter.
  2. Pick your landing rule. Decide whether you mark where the ball first touched the ground or where a fielder would have picked it up. Either works. Mixing them doesn't. For cage work into a net, mark where you think the ball was headed.
  3. Decide on rounds. A common setup is rounds of five to eight swings, with a bunt or two first. Knowing the round size tells you when to move to the next hitter without counting in your head.
  4. Find your spot. Stand where you can see the whole field and the hitter's contact. Behind the cage on the first-base side works for most coaches. If you're also throwing, record between pitches, never during your windup.

The tap rhythm

The goal is one action per ball, done before the next pitch. With paper, that's one dot on a printed diamond. With an app, it's one tap where the ball landed. Then look back up.

A rhythm that works for most coaches: pitch, contact, watch the ball, mark it, next pitch. If you have an extra second, add how hard it was hit. If you don't, skip it. A missing classification is fine. A wrong one is worse than none, because it looks like real information.

Don't let a classification carry over from the last ball out of habit. Each recorded contact stands on its own. If you tapped "line drive" for the previous swing and this one was a ground ball you didn't classify, it should stay unclassified.

Swings and misses matter too

A spray chart only shows balls in play, so it hides a hitter who is swinging through a lot of pitches. If you can, note misses as a separate count. Ten recorded contacts out of twelve swings and ten out of twenty-five are very different rounds, even if the dots look the same.

Handling the messy parts

  • Fouls and bunts: Mark them where they went, behind the foul line or in front of the plate. They're real contact, and a hitter who fouls everything back is telling you about timing.
  • Mis-taps: Fix them right away. Undo the last mark rather than trying to remember it later.
  • Hitters jumping order: It happens when someone's grabbing a helmet. Move them in your order first, then chart, so the balls land under the right name.
  • Machine versus live arm: Note which you used. Machine rounds tend to produce tighter, more pull-heavy charts than live pitching.

How much is enough?

One round tells you almost nothing. A hitter can pull five balls in a row because the pitcher kept missing inside. Patterns start to mean something after roughly 30 to 50 recorded contacts, spread over several practices. That's a week or two of normal BP for most teams.

When you look at a chart, always look at the count next to it. "70% pull" from 10 balls is a hunch. "70% pull" from 60 balls is a pattern worth a conversation.

Keep it sustainable

The best charting system is the one you'll still be using in week eight. If recording every hitter every day is too much, chart one hitting group per practice and rotate. If classifying contact quality slows you down, drop it and just mark locations. Consistency beats detail.
