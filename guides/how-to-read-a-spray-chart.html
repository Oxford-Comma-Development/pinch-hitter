A spray chart is a picture of where a hitter's balls went. It's simple to make and easy to misread. Here's how to get from a cloud of dots to something you can actually coach, without reading more into the chart than it can tell you.

Start with the count, not the picture

Before you look at where the dots are, look at how many there are. Every chart is built from a number of recorded contacts, and that number decides how much you can trust it. Fifteen balls from one Tuesday is a snapshot. Sixty balls across three weeks starts to be a tendency.

Percentages without counts are the most common way spray charts mislead coaches. "Pulls 70%" sounds definitive. "Pulls 7 of 10" sounds like what it is: early. Get in the habit of saying both.

Pull, center, and opposite field

The first pattern to read is direction. Split the field into three slices from home plate:

  • Pull side: left field for a right-handed hitter, right field for a lefty. This is where a hitter who gets the barrel out front naturally hits the ball.
  • Center: a cone straight back up the middle. Pinch Hitter uses 15 degrees on either side of the line through second base.
  • Opposite field: right field for a righty, left field for a lefty. Hitting here usually means letting the ball travel deeper before contact.

Most high-school hitters pull more than they think, especially off a machine or soft toss. A heavy pull lean isn't automatically a problem. It becomes one when the hitter can't do anything else, because a good defense will move to where the balls go.

Quality changes everything

A ground ball to shortstop and a line drive into the left-center gap can sit a few feet apart on a chart. If you recorded contact quality or trajectory, filter or color by it. Two questions do most of the work:

  1. Where do the hard-hit balls go? Many hitters only drive the ball to one side and slap weakly everywhere else. That tells you where their swing works today.
  2. What's the trajectory mix? A chart full of pull-side ground balls often means the hitter is out in front and rolling over. Lots of opposite-field pop ups often mean they're late and getting under it.

Look for clusters and holes

Squint at the chart. Dense clusters show where the swing repeats. Empty zones are just as telling: a right-handed hitter who never puts a ball in right field, even weakly, may not be able to cover the outer half yet.

If your tool has a heatmap view, use it for this. Density makes clusters obvious when individual dots overlap. Remember that a heatmap is derived from the dots. It's a summary, and it carries the same sample-size limits as the chart underneath it.

Compare, don't judge

A single chart is a description. Charts get useful when you compare them:

  • Recent versus season. Did the last two weeks change after you worked on staying through the middle?
  • Against right-handed versus left-handed pitching. Plenty of hitters are a different player against same-side arms.
  • Machine versus live. Machine rounds flatter almost everyone.

Turning a chart into a drill

Once you trust a pattern, pick one thing to change and keep it small. A few examples:

  • Heavy pull, mostly grounders: opposite-field rounds where only balls hit through the right side count, or front toss set slightly outside.
  • Opposite-field pop ups: work on getting the front foot down on time. Short, quick rounds against firm pitching expose late starts.
  • All center, little authority: a hitter may be guiding the ball. Give them a round to just drive it, and see where the hard contact goes.

Then chart the next two weeks and look again. That loop is where charting pays off: you made a change, and you can see whether the dots moved.

What a spray chart can't tell you

Batting practice isn't a game. Pitch speed, location, and intent are all different. A BP chart shows how a hitter swings when they know a strike is coming. Treat it as a coaching tool, not a scouting report, and use it to start conversations with players rather than end them.
